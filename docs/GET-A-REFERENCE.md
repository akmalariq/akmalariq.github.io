## Who can actually give you one

This is the part that changes the plan. **A reference comes from someone who
already knows you, not from a prospect you did work for.**

Look at the 17 published projects — Reels Brain, Dompet, the earthquake
pipeline, the Reels-brain RAG agent. Those are self-directed and product work.
Your Kanmo years are employment, not client work.

**So your references are colleagues and managers, not clients.** That is not a
weakness. For the credible-IC play it is exactly right: *"works well under
production pressure, and was the person we called when it broke"* is the
claim you need, and only a colleague can make it. A client can only speak to
deliverables.

### The sources, ranked by probability

| Rank | Source | Why | Probability |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **A Kanmo colleague or your former manager** | knows you over years, in production, not in a project | high |
| 2 | **Your own manager** | a manager reference is unusual, which is why it is worth more | high, if the relationship is good |
| 3 | **Someone from a project you shipped together** | narrow but real | medium |
| 4 | **An open-source maintainer or community** | only if you have real interactions, not stars | low |
| 5 | **A client** | you do not have one yet — that is why this plan exists | zero, today |

**Do not go to 5.** A reference from someone you are still trying to win is a
reference they will give politely and vaguely. Get the first one from someone
who owes you nothing.

## Ask for a reference, not a testimonial

This is the mechanic that most people get wrong.

A **testimonial** asks for marketing copy. It is work, it is exposed, and a busy
person declines.

A **reference** asks for ten minutes of their time. It is less work than they
expect, and *"sure, ring them"* is a two-word reply that closes more deals than
any paragraph on your website.

### Ask for both, but in this order

**Ask 1 — willing to take a call:**

> Hey — I'm putting my name and some of my work in public for the first time
> and I'm a bit short of people who've worked with me saying so publicly.
>
> Would you be OK if I put your name down as a reference? You don't have to
> write anything. I'll just list that you're happy to take a call, and give
> them your number or email.
>
> If not, genuinely no problem.

**Ask 2 — the sentence, later, once they've said yes to Ask 1.** People who
have agreed to be a reference are dramatically more likely to give you a
sentence, because the hard part (agreeing to be named) is already done.

**Ask 3 — the quote, months later**, after a piece of work they have seen. Only
ask if Ask 1 succeeded and some months have passed. A quote asked for on the
spot is always the weakest version of yourself.

## The ask, precisely

Send **five** of these. Not twenty. Five is a number a person can picture.

> Hi [Name] —
>
> I'm building a solo consulting practice and my website is live for the first
> time. I don't have any client testimonials yet, which is fair, but I do have
> four years of people who worked with me.
>
> Would you be willing to be listed as a reference on the site? Nothing to
> write — I'd just put your name, your role, and that you're happy to be
> contacted. If you know one sentence about how I work that you actually
> believe, I'd use that too, but it has to be true and it has to be yours.
>
> Happy to send you the link so you can see exactly where it would sit.
>
> And if you'd rather not, that's completely fine — you already know.

**Why this works:**
- Names the real problem (no clients yet) instead of implying everyone does
- Offers the easiest version first (a phone call)
- Explicitly invites the honest answer, which lowers the cost of saying yes
- Offers to show exactly where it sits, which removes the "what am I agreeing to" fear
- Never implies they owe you anything
